<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This statement is from the <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7333110996124807168/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">post</a> by Adam DeJans Jr: &#8220;<em>I’ve worked with many companies (from logistics to manufacturing) and one pattern stands out. There’s often too much emphasis on improving forecast accuracy, and too little thought about what really matters: the decisions being made and the cost of being wrong. Instead of asking how far off the forecast was, I ask how much it cost me. I want a metric that reflects business realities. If I miss high on a forecast, what does it do to my bottom line? If I miss low, what opportunities did I lose? The right loss function captures that asymmetry.<br><br>And perhaps most importantly, I never assume my forecasts are correct. <strong>Uncertainty is always part of the problem</strong>. Ignoring it doesn’t make it go away. Every planning system should explicitly account for uncertainty, and every good decision process should be built to handle it.</em>&#8221; <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7333110996124807168/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Link</a></p>
