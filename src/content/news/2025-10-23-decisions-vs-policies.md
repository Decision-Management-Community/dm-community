<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The latest post by <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/addejans/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Adam DeJans Jr.</a> is so important that we decided to reproduce it here. He questions whether we always need a model to make a decision and describes the essential difference between decisions and policies. &#8220;<em>A decision is an action you take given what you know now. A policy is a rule for deciding over time. Decisions are moments. Policies are patterns</em>.&#8221; <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/posts/addejans_what-is-a-decision-what-is-a-policy-years-activity-7386744405753450496-IXLn?utm_source=share&amp;utm_medium=member_desktop&amp;rcm=ACoAAAAFIMoBtgF6zELsOiwFm0hGYH1dIteE6pQ" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Link</a></p>



<!--more-->



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What is a decision? What is a Policy?<br><br>Years ago, I was in a meeting where someone said: “We just need the model to make the decision.”<br><br>Everyone nodded. It sounded visionary.<br>But over time, I realized that sentence hides one of the deepest misunderstandings in analytics.<br><br>Because making a decision and having a policy are not the same thing. And knowing the difference changes how you build every system that’s supposed to “decide.”<br><br>A decision is an action you take given what you know now. A policy is a rule for deciding over time.<br><br>Decisions are moments. Policies are patterns.<br><br>We make decisions every day: assign a truck, build 200 Camrys, reorder parts.<br>But the moment you take one, the world moves. New information appears, uncertainty unfolds, and yesterday’s “optimal” might become tomorrow’s mistake.<br><br>That’s why <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/warrenbpowell/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Warren Powell</a>’s framing of sequential decisions changed how I think.<br>It showed that a decision without context is just an impulse. A policy is a strategy.<br><br>Policies answer the question:<br><br>“Given what I know right now, what should I do every time this situation arises?”<br><br>It’s what humans already do instinctively:<br>&#8211; If it’s raining, bring an umbrella.<br>&#8211; If the data is noisy, use a robust method.<br>&#8211; If the customer hesitates, ask about their pain point.<br><br>In math, we write it as taking action when in state. Simple notation, deep meaning.<br>It captures judgment and not just reaction.<br><br>As <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/vermorel/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Joannes Vermorel</a> often points out, organizations love “decisions” and “data,” but rarely define what good even means or how it changes when reality shifts.<br><br>They chase one-off models and dashboards, like tourists taking pictures instead of learning the landscape.<br><br>Optimization forces harder questions:<br>&#8211; What’s my decision space?<br>&#8211; What state am I really in?<br>&#8211; What happens next if I act this way?<br><br>We need to stop chasing outcomes and start designing systems that decide well repeatedly.<br><br>Building one decision is easy. Building a policy that works over time is hard.<br><br>You must balance:<br>&#8211; Information vs. action<br>&#8211; Now vs. later<br>&#8211; Local vs. global<br>&#8211; Certainty vs. uncertainty<br><br>That’s why frameworks like Sequential Decision Analytics matter; they give language and structure to the dynamics of real problems.<br><br>On one of my early optimization projects, I used to obsess over whether the model made the right call that day.<br><br>Then a supply disruption hit and everything failed. Because we’d built a decision, not a policy.<br><br>Since then, I ask three questions in every project:<br>1. What’s the policy behind this model?<br>2. How does it adapt when the world changes?<br>3. Can it be expressed, audited, and improved?<br><br>That mindset shift, from decision to policy, changed my career.<br><br>Leadership itself is a policy problem. A great leader doesn’t make one good decision. They build systems that keep producing good decisions long after they’re gone.<br><br>That’s the true promise of optimization:<br>Not to make faster decisions, but to design repeatable decision quality.</p>
