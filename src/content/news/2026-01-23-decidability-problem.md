<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;<em>We often assume that if we have good data and a good model, a decision can be made. That assumption quietly fails more often than we think</em>,&#8221; &#8211; wrote <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ronitelman_the-danger-of-assuming-decidability-we-activity-7416517875454107649-FEnP?utm_source=share&amp;utm_medium=member_desktop&amp;rcm=ACoAAAAFIMoBtgF6zELsOiwFm0hGYH1dIteE6pQ">Ron Itelman</a>. &#8220;<em>By <strong>decidability</strong>, I mean a boundary condition: A decision is decidable if the minimum semantic prerequisites required by the decision rule are grounded.</em>&#8221; </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;T<em>ake something trivial: a trading rule that says &#8216;Buy if price today is less than price yesterday.&#8217; The logic is simple. The model works. The data exists. But the system can still halt. Why?<br><br>When a stock-market trader at a London Stock Exchange desk asks their AI agent, &#8220;What were my trades yesterday?&#8221; The system resolves ambiguity before it can answer. Which time zone? &#8220;Yesterday&#8221; from whose perspective: the headquarters in New York, U.S.A. the trader in London, UK or their client at Shenton Way, Singapore? The AI silently decides, then returns a confident answer without exposing those variables to the user.</em>&#8221; <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/context-does-decision-intelligence-need-traces-david-pidsley-oz6me/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Link </a></p>
