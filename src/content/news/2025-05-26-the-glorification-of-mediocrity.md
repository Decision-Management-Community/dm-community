<!--more-->



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>All of this is sold under the comforting label of “simplicity.”<br>But in reality, it’s a distortion, a caricature of the KISS principle.<br><br>Because simple does not mean primitive.<br>Familiar does not mean better.<br>And unfamiliar does not mean complex.<br><br>Telling newcomers to avoid expressive features, to ignore decades of accumulated wisdom and tooling, is not kindness. It’s intellectual laziness disguised as empathy.<br><br>This mindset insists on writing everything in the smallest, most generic subset of imperative programming.<br><br>Why?<br>Because “anything more might scare the beginners.”<br><br>Really?<br>What a stunning vote of no confidence in people’s ability to learn, grow, and rise to challenges.<br><br>We don’t help beginners by shielding them from power.<br>We help them by teaching them how to use it well.<br><br>Because ironically, the code produced under this false “keep it stupidly simple” philosophy is often:<br>→ Harder to read<br>→ Easier to break<br>→ Worse in performance<br>→ Riddled with re-invented wheels<br>→ And a maintenance nightmare<br><br>This isn&#8217;t simplicity.<br>It’s self-inflicted complexity with a friendly face.<br><br>If we want to elevate the craft, and the next generation of developers, we must stop pretending that expressive, idiomatic, well-structured code is “too much” for people to handle.<br><br>Respect the language. Respect the learner. Respect the craft.<br><br>Master your tools. Read the damn docs. Explore the standard library. Use what the language was designed to offer you.<br><br>Because great code isn’t just about making things work.<br>It’s about making things clear, robust, elegant, and built to last.<br><br>We don’t grow by hiding from complexity.<br>We grow by confronting it, and understanding it.</em></p>
