<em><span class="highlight">(5)</span> The sky is so blue.</em><br />
<em><span class="highlight">(7)</span> The sun is so warm up high.</em><br />
<em><span class="highlight">(5)</span> I love the summer.</em></p>
<p>A <a href="https://poets.org/glossary/haiku" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Japanese haiku</a> is a three-line poem with seventeen syllables, written in a <strong>5/7/5</strong> syllable count style.<!--more--></p>
<p>Robert Bosch, known for <a href="http://www.dominoartwork.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Domino Art</a>, proposed to write numerical haikus when numbers read in English, e.g.</p>
<p><strong>      77    </strong>[seventy seven has 5 syllables]
<strong>+ 123    </strong>[one hundred twenty three has  6 syllables + 1 syllable for “plus”]
<strong>= 200   </strong>[two hundred has  3 syllables + 2 syllables for “equals”]
<p>Nathan Brixius’s article “<a href="https://nathanbrixius.wordpress.com/2019/09/22/creating-equation-haikus-using-constraint-programming/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Creating Equation Haikus using Constraint Programming</a>” deals with the question: “<strong>How many such haikus are there?</strong>” He proposed a <a href="https://github.com/natebrix/csp_haiku" target="_blank" rel="noopener">solution</a> and also posted a challenge: “I invite you to modify the code and find other types of equation haikus!”</p>
<p>If you are interested to ponder this challenge and/or create similar “numerical poems” such as <a href="https://poets.org/glossary/tanka" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Tanka</a>, please send your solutions to <a href="mailto:decisionmanagementcommunity@gmail.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener">decisionmanagementcommunity@gmail.com</a>.</p>
