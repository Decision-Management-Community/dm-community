<p><!--more-->When last year, after successful DecisionCAMP-2016 in New York, we decided to hold the next event outside of the US for the very first time, I worried about potential attendance. Will US specialists show up? However, thanks to the serious event preparation work and a great response from the <a href="http://DMCommunity.org" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Decision Management Community</a> worldwide, the actual attendance of DecisionCAMP-2017 proved the London&#8217;s location was a good choice. We received 56 official registrations from US, UK, Canada, Germany, New Zealand, Belgium, Italy, Netherlands, France, Spain, Ireland, and Montenegro. We even needed to switch to a larger auditorium at the Birkbeck University to comfortably fit all attendees. Additionally, people from the co-located RuleML+RR conference and students attended our sessions as well.</p>
<p>But it is not a quantity but the quality of our attendees that really made the difference. World-renowned experts including architects from the major vendors, authors of DMN related books, well-known practitioners and bloggers attended the London &#8216;s camp. We had many members of the DMN Committee who hold their meeting on July 12 as a part of the DecisionCAMP and invited other attendees to attend it. I also received emails from other experts who wanted to attend it but could not make it by personal reasons.</p>

<p>You may find it interesting how these DMN &#8220;likes&#8221; and &#8220;dislikes&#8221; were recorded by <a href="http://jtonedm.com/2017/07/12/decisioncamp-2017-dmn-day/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">James Taylor</a>:</p>
<p><strong>Likes</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Eliciting requirements using decision modeling is more productive, more fun, more rapid than traditional approaches to eliciting requirements.</li>
<li>The use of explicit requirements helps with impact analysis, traceability at a business level.</li>
<li>Really brings together an industry across multiple vendors in a positive way – it’s great to have a standard, customers like the idea that vendors are compliant.</li>
<li>Ability to mix and match analytics, AI, rules, manual decision-making, machine learning, optimization and all other decision-making approaches in a single model.</li>
<li>FEEL has supporters and has some great features – especially for certain activities like defining decision tables, possible for non-technical users to be very precise.</li>
<li>Ability for business users to clearly express their problem and share this with others to get agreement, prompt discussion – and to do this at several levels.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Dislikes</strong>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Perhaps too many details too soon, too much of a focus on the meta model and the internals v what a user can see.</li>
<li>Sometimes the standard is too precise or limiting – not allowing decision tables and output to have different names, for instance.</li>
<li>Dislike some of the corner case features because they can get people into trouble.</li>
<li>Not really any good ways to show which kinds of technology or approach can be used in decision modeling – perhaps some icons.</li>
<li>FEEL has people who don’t like it too, but this is partly because its new and a change and because it perhaps lacks some of the patterns and capabilities needed. More examples would be good too.</li>
</ul>
<p>On July 13 we had a QnA Panel &#8220;<strong>Real-world Business Decision Management: Vendor and Practitioner Perspectives</strong>&#8221; moderated by James Taylor with the following panelists: Gary Hallmark (Oracle), Alain Neyroud (IBM), Nick Broom (DMN practitioner), Jan Purchase (DMN practitioner), Bob Moore (DMN practitioner), Edson Tirelli (Red Hat), Jacob Feldman (OpenRules). Many questions were raised and discussed. Now, 10 days later, my memory subjectively selects only some of them:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Does DMN innovate?</strong> Many panelists agreed that DMN does not bring serious innovations but rather standardizes well-known best practices as it&#8217;s supposed to do</li>
<li><strong>Is DMN here to stay for a long time?</strong> Main answers were: &#8220;Yes, but only for the next 5-10 years while the standard is well accepted&#8221;</li>
<li><strong>Should decision monitoring be a part of DMN? </strong>Gary Hallmark said &#8220;it certainly should&#8221;. My answer was &#8220;No&#8221;: while some vendors (e.g. Sparkling Logic) do a great job with decision execution monitoring, I believe it is more practical to provide an API to allow our users to implement application specific monitoring features that frequently go beyond decision models.</li>
<li><strong>Some experts predict that people will stop purchasing software in the next 5-10 years. What will they purchase?</strong> This tough question was raised by Denis Gagne several times during the Camp, and naturally possible answers were not concrete. I mentioned that our smartest customers sold their software once and then for several years already sell not software updates but rather &#8220;knowledge&#8221; in a form of quarterly updated rules repositories (for example, rules representing the latest best medical practices).  I suspect this is the way to go.</li>
<li><strong>What is the next &#8220;killer&#8221; application for Decision Management?</strong> I don&#8217;t remember hearing any specific answer. I just noticed that we probably should look for an answer at our co-located conference &#8220;<a href="http://2017.ruleml-rr.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">RuleML+RR</a>&#8221; where semantic web people already have created good reasoning tools. If we consider our decision models as &#8220;decision ontologies&#8221; and apply similar reasoning tools to them, we would not need to force our customers to write specific rules that lead to calculation of insurance premiums or acceptance/rejection of loan applications. Having rules to define all relationships inside our inter-related decision models (ontologies), they (users) would only need to specify the goal (e.g. insurance premium), and then a <strong>Decision Reasoner</strong> will automatically calculate it for a particular insurance policy.</li>
</ul>
