<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In his recent article &#8220;<a href="https://www.brcommunity.com/articles.php?id=c134">Being Unambiguous Beyond Reasonable Doubt in Expressing Rules</a>&#8221; Ron Ross gave an example of the kind of ambiguity that policy interpreters, business analysts, and IT professionals deal with daily. It&#8217;s a sentence from the California 2014 Paid Sick Leave Policy: &#8220;<em>Accrued paid sick leave shall carry over to the following year of employment and may be capped at 48 hours or 6 days.</em>&#8221; Let&#8217;s look at the ambiguities: </p>



<!--more-->



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>What is being capped? Is it the amount of accrued paid sick leave in a given period or the amount of sick leave that may be carried forward?</li>



<li>What is meant by &#8220;may be&#8221;?&nbsp;<em>… Must be? … Can be but need not be?</em></li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These ambiguities must be wrung out of the statement to get a clear interpretation (i.e., unambiguous beyond a reasonable doubt). The key to disambiguation is forming the right questions to ask. A good reader of formal messages &#8216;sees&#8217; the questions that should be asked. A good writer of formal messages avoids them in the first place.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Ron states: &#8220;By &#8216;being unambiguous&#8217; I do <em>not</em> mean being unambiguous beyond all doubt in every aspect of natural language. <em>No! </em>I stipulate up-front that&#8217;s impossible (and probably not even desirable). By &#8216;being unambiguous&#8217; I mean removing ambiguity <em>beyond a reasonable doubt</em> in formal communication including rules.&#8221; <a href="https://www.brcommunity.com/articles.php?id=c134" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Link</a></p>
