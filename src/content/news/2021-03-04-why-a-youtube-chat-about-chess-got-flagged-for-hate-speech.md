<p>This WIRED <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/why-youtube-chat-chess-flagged-hate-speech/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">article</a> talks about shortcomings in AI programs designed to automatically detect hate speech, abuse, and misinformation online. When WIRED fed some of statements gathered by the CMU researchers into two hate-speech classifiers, the statement “<em>White’s attack on black is brutal. White is stomping all over black’s defenses. The black king is gonna fall…</em> ” was judged more than 60 percent likely to be hate speech. “<em>Fundamentally, language is still a very subtle thing</em>,” says <a class="external-link" href="http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~tom/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Tom Mitchell</a>, a CMU professor. “<em>These kinds of trained classifiers are not soon going to be 100 percent accurate</em>.” <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/why-youtube-chat-chess-flagged-hate-speech/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Link</a></p>
