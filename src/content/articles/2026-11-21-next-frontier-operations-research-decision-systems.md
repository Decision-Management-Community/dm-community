## In brief

Optimization performance, policy performance, and decision-system performance are three different things. An optimization model can solve its mathematical problem efficiently while producing decisions that are disappointing in operation. A policy can perform well when evaluated on its own, yet degrade when another part of the organization changes its policy. Even an apparently strong combination can fail after deployment if the evaluation ignores feedback, delayed consequences, correlated uncertainty, or operational constraints. These distinctions matter because most organizations do not operate isolated models. They operate interconnected decisions that repeatedly change the states in which subsequent decisions are made.

The implication is that we need several levels of evidence. We should continue to test individual policies, because controlled experiments help identify what a component contributes and why. We should also test combinations of policies in a shared environment, preserving the state transitions and economic consequences that couple their actions. Ultimately, the most meaningful evaluation concerns the behavior of the complete decision system over time, not simply the quality of any particular solver result or the standalone score of any particular policy.

## Why solver performance is an incomplete measurement

Mathematical optimization gives us a powerful language for specifying decisions, constraints, and objectives. Within that language, solver performance is often measured through runtime, feasible incumbents, optimality gaps, numerical reliability, and the strength of the final bound. These are important engineering and mathematical properties. When a mixed-integer model is well formulated and its objective adequately captures the decision being made, faster and more reliable solving can create substantial value. There is no reason to dismiss improvements to optimization algorithms merely because the surrounding business system is complicated.

But a solver certificate establishes a property of a specified mathematical model. It does not certify that the model is the right economic abstraction, that its input distributions describe the future accurately, or that the organization will execute the recommended actions. The objective may omit costs that are paid elsewhere. A constraint may encode a simplification that was once acceptable but is now materially wrong. A decision may be optimal for one time period while shifting costs into future periods or into another organizational unit. These are failures of system representation and evaluation, not necessarily failures of the solver.

It is therefore useful to distinguish three nested questions. Did we solve the mathematical problem we wrote down? Does the resulting policy perform well across the states and uncertainty it is likely to encounter? Does that policy improve the larger system once its actions interact with other decisions, physical processes, and future information? Each question requires a different kind of evidence, and answering the first does not automatically settle the second or third.

## A policy is a mechanism that repeatedly produces actions

A production decision system rarely solves a single static problem and then disappears. It observes information, estimates some representation of the current state, chooses an action, modifies the operating environment, and repeats. A policy is the mechanism that maps the information available at a decision point to an action. It might be a simple reorder rule, a model predictive controller, a mathematical program solved repeatedly, a parameterized cost-function approximation, a learned policy, or a mixture of methods with explicit fallback behavior.

This perspective changes what we mean by evaluating a decision. The quality of a policy cannot be established from a single favorable action or one optimal mathematical objective. Its actions alter inventory positions, capacity commitments, queue lengths, customer promises, vendor incentives, and the future options available to the business. A policy that reduces immediate purchasing cost could increase later expediting costs. A policy that raises local fill rate could consume shared stock required by another node. A rule that is conservative in an isolated experiment could become destabilizing when another rule responds aggressively to the same signal.

The policy is also broader than its central algorithm. State estimation, data freshness, feature definitions, trigger frequencies, exception handling, and execution rules affect the action that actually occurs. Two services calling the same solver can implement economically different policies if one sees delayed inventory data, applies stale lead times, or overrides the recommendation during execution. A technically credible evaluation must make these differences explicit rather than treating the optimization call as the whole decision mechanism.

## The manufacturer, warehouse, and marketplace example

Consider a stylized supply chain with manufacturers replenishing a warehouse and the warehouse replenishing several marketplaces. The upstream buying policy may choose when and how much to order from suppliers with long production and transportation lead times, minimum order quantities, and fixed ordering cadences. The downstream distribution policy may use shorter replenishment lead times, local inventory positions, service targets, and allocation rules to move available stock toward anticipated customer demand. Each policy solves a real decision problem, and each can be improved independently within its own operating context.

Suppose we improve the upstream policy by reducing excess inventory while maintaining an acceptable warehouse-level service measure. In a separate experiment, we improve the downstream policy by making it more responsive to changes in marketplace demand. Both isolated experiments might show economic gains. The upstream policy reduces buffers and commits inventory less aggressively; the downstream policy reallocates inventory faster and becomes more sensitive to local shortages. Once combined, however, aggressive downstream reactions can amplify demand signals seen by upstream buying. The upstream policy may then face larger swings in requested replenishment precisely when it has less inventory available to absorb them.

The result could be more frequent allocation conflicts, ordering variability, supplier constraints, and costly recovery actions than either isolated experiment suggested. The opposite interaction is possible as well. A smoother downstream policy may reduce the uncertainty that upstream buying needs to hedge, allowing the combination to outperform the sum of its standalone gains. The point is not that one of these effects must occur. The point is that interactions are properties of the coupled system and cannot be inferred reliably from two separately optimized objective values.

This is why I would not eliminate individual policy testing. It is valuable for diagnosis, attribution, regression testing, and initial screening. I would eliminate the assumption that two independent improvements add together cleanly. The final comparison must include the original upstream and downstream policies, each proposed change paired with the original counterpart, and the combined changes. That experimental structure makes the interaction visible instead of burying it inside an unexplained network-level result.

## Shared state is what makes the decisions inseparable

Coupling enters through state. The supplier's production commitments influence inbound availability, which changes warehouse inventory, which constrains marketplace replenishment, which changes future lost sales and observed demand. Today's downstream allocations can determine which products will still be available tomorrow. Today's upstream purchases determine future capacity consumption and cash tied up in inventory. If the evaluation resets every period to a convenient initial condition, it removes the mechanism through which decisions produce delayed consequences.

Lead times are particularly important because actions are taken against information that will be stale when the results arrive. A manufacturing order may take months to become usable stock, while a warehouse transfer may take days or weeks. That difference creates multiple overlapping decision cycles, not one synchronized optimization problem. The warehouse must react while prior manufacturing decisions are still in flight; the manufacturer must plan without knowing all the downstream states it will eventually serve. The quality of an upstream policy depends partly on how downstream decisions will exploit or exhaust the inventory it makes available.

Realistic simulation therefore needs to carry forward inventory, open orders, production commitments, backlogs, capacity, and other relevant state variables. It must also preserve the sequence in which information becomes available. Giving a policy tomorrow's realized demand while pretending it operates today creates an advantage that no production system can reproduce. The experiment should distinguish the demand generating process from the observations available to the policy, and it should evaluate actions using the same information timing they would have after deployment.

## Uncertainty must preserve the structure that matters to decisions

Monte Carlo simulation is often presented as a matter of drawing many uncertain futures and averaging outcomes. The number of paths matters for statistical precision, but the structure of those paths matters for validity. Independent samples that produce implausible jumps from one period to the next may misrepresent how a buying or replenishment policy actually experiences demand. Similarly, drawing each marketplace independently can erase cross-market correlations that determine the value of pooled inventory and allocation flexibility.

For the upstream ordering problem, temporal dependence is especially consequential. Sustained demand increases, persistent forecast bias, promotions, seasonality, and product lifecycle changes affect lead-time demand differently from isolated random spikes. Two demand models can have similar single-period means and variances but generate materially different inventory requirements because one produces long runs of high demand while the other frequently reverses direction. A policy evaluated against the latter may look artificially resilient if the real risk involves persistent deviations over several weeks.

The uncertainty model should therefore reflect the dependencies that materially influence decisions. That can require correlated innovations over time, common demand factors across marketplaces, shared supplier disruptions, and lead-time variation that is connected to operating conditions. Perfect realism is neither achievable nor necessary, but important dependencies cannot be discarded merely because independent sampling is easier. The model should be validated through features such as multiweek demand accumulation, the distribution of run lengths, cross-location dependence, and the frequency of capacity or service stress events.

A second issue is experimental fairness. When comparing two policies, it is often useful to run both against the same underlying scenario paths so that differences in outcomes are less obscured by sampling noise. Common random numbers are a variance-reduction technique, not a guarantee of causal identification or correctness. State-dependent processes and policy-dependent random-number consumption need careful implementation so the comparison retains a meaningful shared environment. Every reported improvement should include uncertainty around the estimated economic difference and enough detail to reproduce the experiment.

## Build the experiment around interactions, not just winners

Suppose a team proposes changes to the manufacturer buying policy and the warehouse allocation policy. A disciplined first study can evaluate the baseline system, the upstream change alone, the downstream change alone, and both changes together, all under a common scenario design. The difference between the combined result and what the two standalone changes would predict is evidence of interaction. This is a more informative question than asking only which of the four alternatives has the highest estimated objective in a particular batch of simulated futures.

That basic design can be extended to more components, but a full factorial experiment becomes expensive as the number of policies increases. We can use screening designs, carefully selected combinations, surrogate models, or sequential experimentation to identify which interactions justify further evaluation. We should not assume the policy space is separable, and we should not assume every possible coupling is important. The role of experimentation is to learn which dependencies matter enough to change a decision, then allocate computational effort accordingly.

When a candidate performs poorly in the combined system, we should trace the effect through operational mechanisms. Did it create larger inbound fluctuations, concentrate stockouts in one marketplace, trigger additional expedited orders, increase capacity saturation, or move cost from one ledger to another? An aggregate profit number is essential for ranking business outcomes, but it cannot by itself explain why the difference occurred. Diagnostic measures should be chosen to illuminate the state transitions and constraints that connect one policy to another.

## Optimize the shared economics rather than negotiating proxy metrics

Organizational boundaries commonly create different objective functions for connected decisions. An upstream team might optimize purchase cost, inbound utilization, or warehouse availability. A downstream team might optimize local service, speed, or sales. Those measures can be useful operational signals, but optimizing them independently may create a network that is economically inconsistent. Internal transfers, inventory ownership conventions, and accounting allocations can make costs appear in different places even when the underlying physical consequence is unchanged.

A system-level evaluation requires a consistent economic ledger. The ledger should reflect the outcomes that actually change with the policy: sales margin, procurement, handling, transportation, holding costs, shortage consequences, expediting, disposals, and relevant capacity penalties. Transfer prices between internal nodes should not be counted as though they create external economic value. Where customer-service or reliability commitments cannot credibly be monetized, they can remain explicit constraints or reported objectives rather than being hidden inside arbitrary penalty weights.

This does not require a single enormous mathematical program that directly optimizes every decision at once. Separate decision services may be entirely appropriate for operational, organizational, or computational reasons. The critical issue is whether their objectives and interfaces support coherent system behavior. We can retain modular policies while evaluating and tuning them against shared downstream consequences. In some settings, joint policy search will be worth the additional complexity; in others, a sequence of coordinated local changes with repeated system-level validation will capture much of the value.

## What policy search should actually search

Once performance is defined at the system level, the search space can include more than individual tuning parameters. It can include policy families, decision cadences, information-sharing rules, inventory allocation logic, economic penalties, and the boundaries between automated and manual intervention. A fixed rule may be sufficient at one node while a mathematical program is justified at another. A policy with a small number of transparent parameters may beat a more elaborate learned controller after accounting for data requirements, operational stability, and maintenance costs.

There is no general reason to favor mixed-integer optimization, dynamic programming, reinforcement learning, approximate dynamic programming, or simple heuristics without considering the available structure. Mixed-integer programming may express discrete operational constraints exceptionally well while requiring careful treatment of the future. Dynamic programming can explicitly account for state transitions when the state space and action structure are manageable. Reinforcement learning can be useful when repeated feedback and an adequate training environment permit learning a policy directly. Cost-function approximations and rolling-horizon optimization often provide a practical middle ground. The appropriate architecture depends on what decisions repeat, what state is observable, what uncertainty matters, and what guarantees the organization needs.

It is also possible to use simulation as the system-level evaluator while applying several methods within individual policy modules. The simulator then creates a common operational and economic test bench. A candidate may solve one internal optimization problem more slowly yet improve overall outcomes by avoiding costly future states. Another candidate may give up a small amount of local objective value in exchange for more reliable execution, lower computation cost, or greater robustness. These are legitimate tradeoffs when measured at the level where the business actually experiences them.

## Avoid learning the simulator's mistakes

A simulation-based selection process can be very effective at exploiting imperfections in the simulator. Repeatedly tuning against one scenario generator, one historical period, or one implementation of business logic creates a risk analogous to overfitting a predictive model. The selected policy may become excellent at navigating the assumptions of the test environment while remaining fragile to dynamics that were simplified or excluded. This is especially dangerous when the tuning process evaluates many alternatives and reports only the highest observed score.

A stronger process separates development scenarios from held-out validation scenarios and includes stress tests that deliberately challenge the presumed distribution. Candidate policies should be compared across changes in demand persistence, supplier reliability, lead-time volatility, cost assumptions, and execution constraints. In some cases, evaluating multiple plausible models of the environment provides more useful evidence than spending all additional compute to narrow confidence intervals under one model. Robustness is not simply a larger Monte Carlo sample. It is evidence that a recommendation survives meaningful uncertainty about how the system itself behaves.

Deployment creates another validation stage. Controlled rollout, shadow evaluation, intervention logging, and post-decision outcome measurement can reveal differences between simulation and production. Because policies change system states, a production comparison must consider interference between treatments, delayed outcomes, and the possibility that one population's decisions affect another. An online experiment that ignores shared inventory or capacity may violate the independence assumptions that make an ordinary randomized comparison easy to interpret. The experimental design should reflect the physical coupling already recognized in the model.

## Verification belongs at every layer

A defensible decision system needs different tests for different failure modes. At the mathematical level, verify model construction, units, constraints, solver status, and the reproducibility of objective calculations. At the policy level, verify information timing, action feasibility, exception handling, and sensitivity to the state that is observable at decision time. At the simulator level, test conservation of inventory, order-flow accounting, lead-time mechanics, event ordering, and the behavior of extreme scenarios. At the economic level, reconcile the objective with operational measures and ensure that the same consequence is not counted twice.

System-level tests should then evaluate composition. If one policy changes the distribution of states encountered by another, the integration test should expose that dependency. If the downstream policy requests inventory that the upstream system cannot supply, the simulator should enforce the shortage instead of silently creating stock. If two modules issue competing claims on capacity, the allocation rules should resolve the conflict according to the intended business logic. A set of individually correct components can still produce an incorrect or unstable system when combined, and the test suite should be designed to catch that class of failure.

The distinction between verification and validation is useful here. Verification asks whether the code and model implement the intended rules correctly. Validation asks whether those rules and assumptions represent the real decision environment well enough to support the recommendation. An optimization model can pass verification while failing validation. Conversely, a realistic simulator can be paired with a flawed policy implementation. Treating these questions separately keeps a solver's mathematical credibility from being stretched into a claim about unobserved business reality.

## From the Decision Factory to systems of interacting policies

In *The Decision Factory*, the central idea is that organizations create value through a repeatable process that turns information into decisions and decisions into operational outcomes. Forecasting, optimization, simulation, execution, and feedback have to work together if analytical capability is going to change what the business actually does. That perspective remains useful, and nothing in the argument here requires abandoning component-level modeling, individual policy comparisons, or disciplined policy search.

The next step is to treat the interactions among those components as an explicit research subject. We should ask which policy is best, but also which other policies it assumes, which states it creates, which economic consequences it shifts, and how its apparent advantage changes when the rest of the system adapts. A good production decision architecture should make these questions answerable. It should expose policy interfaces, record decisions and realized states, support counterfactual experiments, and preserve enough operational detail to explain disagreements between isolated and integrated evaluations.

The progression is from optimizing individual decisions, to searching for better policies, to engineering and evaluating the system that produces and executes those policies. The third level does not replace the first two. It gives them context and determines whether their improvements survive contact with the connected business. That is where the most important optimization opportunities may be hiding: not inside a single solver call, but in the behavior of the decision system as a whole.
